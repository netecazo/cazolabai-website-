## Learning objectives

1. Recognise a critical value and notify the right person within the required time.
2. Use read-back and document the notification completely.
3. Explain what delta checks catch and investigate a delta failure.
4. Spot results that don't make physiological sense before releasing them.
5. Know when a result should be held, commented or recollected instead of reported.

## Lesson

### 1. Critical values

A critical (panic) value is a result that suggests a life-threatening condition needing prompt action. Each lab sets its own list and limits with its medical staff. **[CHECK: add the pilot lab's chemistry critical value table.]**

When you get one:

1. **Check it makes sense first.** Specimen integrity (haemolysis, clots, contamination), QC status, instrument flags, patient history. Don't delay a real critical value to over-investigate, but don't phone a spurious one either.
2. **Notify** the responsible licensed caregiver (or the person your policy names) within your lab's time limit.
3. **Read-back:** the person receiving the result repeats back the patient identifiers and the result. You confirm it's correct.
4. **Document:** result, patient, who you notified (name and role), date and time, that read-back was done, and your identity.
5. **Escalate** if you can't reach anyone within the time limit, per the escalation path in your policy.

**[CHECK: pilot lab's notification time limit and escalation path; accreditor requirements for critical result communication.]**

### 2. Delta checks

A delta check compares a result with the same patient's previous result within a set time window. A large, unexpected change triggers review.

What a delta failure can mean:

- **Wrong blood in tube (WBIT) / mislabel.** Several unrelated analytes change at once, often in ways that don't fit together.
- **Specimen contamination** (IV fluid, EDTA, wrong tube).
- **Specimen problem** (haemolysis, delayed processing).
- **Analytical error.**
- **Real clinical change** (dialysis, transfusion, treatment, surgery). Most delta failures are real. The check makes you think, not reject.

Investigate before releasing: look at the whole panel, the specimen, QC, the patient's location and history. If you suspect a mislabel, **don't report**; request a recollection and follow your procedure for misidentified specimens.

### 3. Results that don't make sense

Learn the patterns that tell you the specimen is wrong, not the patient:

| Pattern | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| High K, very low Ca, low ALP (and Mg) | **EDTA contamination** (EDTA chelates Ca and the metal cofactors ALP needs) |
| Very high glucose, other analytes diluted (low Na, Cl, Ca, protein) | **Dextrose IV fluid** contamination |
| High Na and Cl, other analytes diluted | **Saline** contamination |
| High K, LD, AST, phosphate with visible haemolysis | **Haemolysis** |
| High K with normal haemolysis index, high platelet or WBC count | **Pseudohyperkalaemia** (cell release in clotting); consider plasma or whole-blood K |
| Glucose falling in a sample that sat unspun for hours | **Glycolysis** |

**[CHECK: add the pilot lab's own common patterns and how its LIS flags them.]**

### 4. Before you release

- Instrument flags cleared or understood
- QC acceptable for the run
- Specimen indices (HIL) within limits or commented
- Critical values notified and documented
- Delta failures reviewed
- Results fit together physiologically

## Question bank

**Pass mark: 80%. [CHECK]**

1. **What must happen during a critical value phone call besides giving the result?**
   **Answer:** Read-back of patient identifiers and result by the receiver, confirmed by you.

2. **List what must be documented for a critical value notification.**
   **Answer:** Result, patient, person notified (name/role), date and time, read-back confirmed, who notified.

3. **You can't reach the nurse or physician within the time limit. What do you do?**
   **Answer:** Follow the escalation policy (charge nurse, attending, medical director, etc.) and document every attempt.

4. **Potassium 7.2, calcium 3.1 mg/dL, ALP 12 U/L on an outpatient with previously normal results. Most likely cause?**
   **Answer:** EDTA contamination. Don't report as is; request recollection.

5. **Glucose 1100 mg/dL, sodium 124, chloride 88, calcium 6.8, total protein 4.1; the patient has a D5 drip. Most likely cause?**
   **Answer:** Dextrose IV fluid contamination (sample drawn above the drip). Recollect from the other arm.

6. **What's a delta check?**
   **Answer:** Comparison with the patient's previous result; a large unexpected change triggers review.

7. **Name three possible explanations for a delta failure.**
   **Answer (any three):** mislabel/WBIT, contamination, specimen problem, analytical error, real clinical change.

8. **Most delta failures turn out to be...**
   **Answer:** Real clinical changes. The check prompts review; it isn't an automatic rejection.

9. **You suspect a specimen belongs to a different patient. What do you do?**
   **Answer:** Don't release results. Notify per policy, request recollection, and document as a suspected misidentified specimen.

10. **Potassium 6.4 with a high haemolysis index. Report it as a critical?**
    **Answer:** Follow policy: usually report with a haemolysis comment (or suppress K) and request recollection; don't phone it as a true critical without qualification.

11. **Why check QC before phoning a critical value?**
    **Answer:** A failed run makes the result unreliable; you'd be phoning a number you can't stand behind.

12. **A sample sat unspun for six hours. Which result is falsely low?**
    **Answer:** Glucose (glycolysis). Potassium may be falsely high.

## Case: potassium 6.9

At 05:10 an inpatient's potassium is 6.9 mmol/L (critical). Yesterday it was 4.1. The haemolysis index is within limits. Sodium 139, calcium 5.9 mg/dL (yesterday 9.2), ALP 18 U/L (yesterday 88). The specimen came from the ward in a gold-top tube; the label is correct. QC is acceptable.

**Questions**
1. Is this a true critical potassium?
2. What pattern do you see?
3. What do you do?
4. What do you document?

**Model answer**
1. Probably not. The pattern suggests contamination.
2. High K, sharply low Ca and low ALP compared with yesterday, no haemolysis: **EDTA contamination** (e.g. wrong order of draw, or decanting from a lavender tube).
3. Don't release K, Ca and ALP (or release with a comment per policy). Call the ward: explain the suspected contamination and request an immediate recollection with correct order of draw. If the clinical team is worried about true hyperkalaemia, recollection is the fastest safe route.
4. Results withheld, reason, who you spoke to (name/role), time, recollection requested, recollection result. Follow up with the ward on the collection issue.
