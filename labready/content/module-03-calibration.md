## Lesson

### 1. What calibration does

Calibration sets the relationship between the signal the analyser measures (absorbance, voltage, light units) and the concentration it reports. Calibrators have assigned values traceable to a reference. The instrument measures them and builds a factor or curve.

QC checks that the relationship still holds. Calibration creates it. If the calibration is wrong, every result is wrong in the same direction, and QC will usually show it as a systematic shift.

### 2. When to calibrate

Follow the manufacturer's instructions first. Typical triggers:

- A new reagent lot (on many systems)
- The calibration interval set by the manufacturer or your lab has expired
- The reagent pack or cassette calibration interval has expired (on-board or pack calibration)
- After major maintenance or replacement of a critical part (lamp, electrode, probe)
- QC shows a shift or trend and the investigation points to calibration
- The analyser prompts or flags a calibration as required

### 3. Reading a calibration

Before accepting a calibration, look at:

- **Instrument flags or errors.** A calibration with a flag isn't accepted until you understand it.
- **Replicate agreement.** Duplicates of each calibrator should agree closely. Poor agreement points to random error: bubbles, probe, mixing, short volume.
- **Factor, slope or signal compared with previous calibrations.** A big jump from the last good calibration means something changed: calibrator, reagent, instrument.
- **Calibrator handling.** Right lot, within expiry, reconstituted with the right diluent and volume, mixed and allowed to stand as instructed, not left open.

After a calibration is accepted, **run all QC levels** before reporting patients. A calibration isn't proven until QC confirms it.

### 4. Common calibration problems

| What you see | Look at |
|---|---|
| Calibration fails with poor replicate agreement | Bubbles, probe, clots, calibrator volume, mixing |
| Calibration "passes" but QC shifts afterwards | Calibrator lot or value entry, reconstitution, reagent |
| Factor very different from last time | Calibrator reconstitution or lot, reagent deterioration, lamp/electrode |
| Repeated calibration failures after a reagent lot change | New reagent lot, lot-specific calibrator values, contact manufacturer |
| Calibration drifts and needs repeating early | Reagent on-board stability, electrode ageing, temperature |

Recalibrating repeatedly until it passes is the calibration version of rerunning QC until it passes. Find the cause.

### 5. Calibration verification

**Calibration verification** confirms that the calibration still gives accurate results across the reportable range. Under CLIA it's performed by testing materials of known value at a minimum of three levels (low, mid and high) across the reportable range and checking the results fall within the lab's acceptance limits.

It's required **at least every six months** and whenever:

- all reagents are changed to a new lot, unless the lab can show the change doesn't affect the range of results
- major preventive maintenance is done or a critical part is replaced
- QC shows an unusual trend or shift, or is outside limits, and other means haven't resolved it
- the lab's own procedure calls for it more often

If the lab calibrates with at least three levels covering the reportable range at least every six months, that calibration can meet the requirement.

Calibration verification material is often the same set used to verify the analytical measurement range (Module 4).

### 6. Documentation

Record for every calibration: test, instrument, date/time, calibrator lot and expiry, reagent lot, result (factor, slope, signals), any flags, accept/reject, QC after calibration, tech. For calibration verification: material and lot, each level's expected and measured value, acceptance limits, pass/fail, reviewer.

## Question bank

**Pass mark: 80%. [CHECK]**

1. **What's the difference between calibration and QC?**
   **Answer:** Calibration sets the signal-to-concentration relationship. QC checks that it still holds.

2. **You've just recalibrated creatinine and it passed. What must happen before patient results are reported?**
   **Answer:** Run all QC levels and confirm they're acceptable.

3. **A calibration fails with poor agreement between calibrator replicates. What kind of error, and name two causes.**
   **Answer:** Random error. Any two of: bubbles, clot or probe problem, short calibrator volume, poor mixing.

4. **The new calibration factor is 25% different from the last good one. What do you do?**
   **Answer:** Don't accept it without investigating: check calibrator lot, reconstitution and expiry, reagent condition, instrument flags; compare with calibration history.

5. **Name three situations that require calibration verification besides the six-month interval.**
   **Answer (any three):** complete reagent lot change (unless shown not to affect results); major maintenance or critical part replaced; QC shift or trend unresolved by other means; lab procedure requires it.

6. **What is the minimum number of levels used for calibration verification, and where must they fall?**
   **Answer:** Three: low, mid and high, covering the reportable range.

7. **True or false: if QC is in range, calibration verification isn't needed.**
   **Answer:** False. It's a separate requirement with its own frequency.

8. **A tech recalibrates four times until a calibration passes. What's wrong?**
   **Answer:** Repeating hides the cause. After a failed calibration, investigate calibrator, reagent and instrument before recalibrating.

9. **After replacing the photometer lamp, what should you do before reporting?**
   **Answer:** Follow the manufacturer's steps (lamp checks, recalibration if required), run all QC levels, and perform calibration verification if required by your procedure.

10. **The calibrator was reconstituted with 5.0 mL instead of 3.0 mL. What happens to patient results after calibration?**
    **Answer:** The calibrator is diluted, so it produces a lower signal for its assigned value. The system then over-reports: patients read falsely **high**. QC should show a positive shift.

11. **What should a calibration record contain? Name five items.**
    **Answer (any five):** test, instrument, date/time, calibrator lot/expiry, reagent lot, result/factor, flags, accept/reject, post-calibration QC, tech.

12. **Why compare the calibration factor with previous calibrations?**
    **Answer:** A large change signals something different in calibrator, reagent or instrument even if the calibration technically passes.

## Case: creatinine after a pack change

A new creatinine reagent pack was loaded at 22:00 and the analyser required a pack calibration. It passed without flags. At the 23:00 QC, both levels are about +2.3 SD (2-2s). The calibration factor is about 12% different from the last three calibrations, which agreed closely. The calibrator vial was reconstituted by the evening tech at 21:45.

**Questions**
1. What type of error and what's the most likely area?
2. What do you do first?
3. Which details of the calibration do you check?
4. How do you fix and verify?
5. What goes in the record?

**Model answer**
1. Systematic, all levels high after a calibration. Calibration is the prime suspect.
2. Hold creatinine patient results since the calibration and notify the charge tech.
3. Calibrator lot and expiry; reconstitution volume and diluent (a diluted calibrator makes patients read high); time allowed to stand; whether values were entered or read correctly; reagent pack condition.
4. Reconstitute a fresh calibrator correctly, recalibrate, confirm the factor is back in line with history, run all QC levels. Look back from the calibration at 22:00 and rerun released creatinine results per policy.
5. Event, factor comparison, cause found (e.g. calibrator reconstituted with the wrong volume), fresh calibrator and recalibration, post-calibration QC, look-back outcome, notification, follow-up (e.g. reconstitution double-check at shift change).
