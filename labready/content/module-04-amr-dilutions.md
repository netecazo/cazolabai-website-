## Learning objectives

1. Define the analytical measurement range (AMR) and the clinically reportable range (CRR).
2. Handle a result above the AMR: automatic and manual dilution, correct factor, correct reporting.
3. Handle a result below the AMR without extrapolating.
4. Explain how the AMR is verified and how often.
5. Recognise dilution errors and the hook effect.

## Lesson

### 1. Two ranges

- **Analytical measurement range (AMR):** the range of values the method can measure directly on the specimen, without dilution, concentration or other pretreatment that isn't part of the usual process.
- **Clinically reportable range (CRR):** the range of values the lab reports, including results obtained after a validated dilution. It can extend above the AMR (by dilution) but never below the lower AMR limit without a validated method.

Your procedure or LIS should list both for every test. **[CHECK: add the pilot lab's AMR and CRR table for the chemistry menu.]**

### 2. Results above the AMR

1. The analyser flags the result as above range. **Don't report the flagged number.**
2. Dilute: automatic rerun with the analyser's validated dilution, or a manual dilution per procedure.
3. Use only the **diluent** and **dilution factors** your lab has validated for that test.
4. The diluted result must fall **within the AMR** before multiplying. If it's still above, dilute further, up to the maximum validated dilution.
5. Multiply by the dilution factor (the analyser does this for auto-dilutions; check the manual ones).
6. If the result is still above range at the maximum validated dilution, report as **greater than** the upper CRR limit (e.g. "> 4000 U/L").
7. Document the dilution.

**Dilution factor** = total volume ÷ sample volume. 1 part sample + 4 parts diluent = 1:5, factor 5.

### 3. Results below the AMR

Report as **less than** the lower limit (e.g. "< 2 mg/dL"). Never extrapolate below the lowest point verified. You can't concentrate a sample on a routine chemistry analyser to get a number.

### 4. Common dilution errors

- Wrong factor applied, or applied twice (manual factor on top of an auto-dilution already multiplied)
- Wrong diluent (water instead of the specified diluent for some assays)
- Pipetting error, especially with small volumes
- Diluted result still above AMR but reported anyway
- Diluted result very low near the bottom of the AMR, magnifying imprecision. Choose a dilution that lands mid-range.

### 5. Hook effect (immunoassays)

In some sandwich immunoassays, a very high analyte concentration can saturate the antibodies and give a **falsely low** result. If a result doesn't fit the clinical picture (e.g. a very large tumour with a modest tumour marker), a dilution that gives a **higher** corrected result reveals it. Know which of your assays are susceptible. **[CHECK: list the pilot lab's susceptible assays, per manufacturer.]**

### 6. Verifying the AMR

The lab verifies the AMR by testing materials with known values at the low end, midpoint and high end of the range, and checking the recovery against acceptance limits. Many accreditors require AMR verification at least every six months and after major changes, and calibration verification materials often serve both purposes. **[CHECK: confirm frequency and material requirements against the pilot lab's accreditor checklist.]**

Validated maximum dilutions and the CRR are set during method validation and approved by the laboratory director.

## Question bank

**Pass mark: 80%. [CHECK]**

1. **Define AMR.**
   **Answer:** The range the method measures directly without dilution or pretreatment outside the normal process.

2. **How does the CRR differ from the AMR?**
   **Answer:** The CRR is what's reported, including validated dilutions, so it can extend above the AMR.

3. **You mix 50 µL of serum with 450 µL of diluent. What's the dilution factor?**
   **Answer:** 500 ÷ 50 = 10.

4. **After a 1:10 manual dilution, lipase reads 180 U/L. What do you report?**
   **Answer:** 1800 U/L (if 180 is within the AMR and the dilution is validated).

5. **A diluted sample still reads above the AMR. What do you do?**
   **Answer:** Dilute further up to the maximum validated dilution; if still above, report as greater than the upper CRR limit.

6. **Glucose reads below the lower AMR limit of 2 mg/dL. The analyser displays 1.2. What do you report?**
   **Answer:** "< 2 mg/dL." Never report an extrapolated value.

7. **An auto-dilution result is 2400 U/L. A tech multiplies by 5 again. What's wrong?**
   **Answer:** The analyser already applied the factor. The reported value would be five times too high.

8. **Why choose a dilution that puts the diluted result mid-range rather than near the bottom of the AMR?**
   **Answer:** Imprecision near the lower limit gets multiplied by the factor.

9. **What is the hook effect and how do you detect it?**
   **Answer:** Very high antigen concentration in some sandwich immunoassays gives a falsely low result. A dilution that gives a higher corrected result reveals it.

10. **Can you use deionised water for any dilution?**
    **Answer:** Only if it's the validated diluent for that test. Use what the procedure specifies.

11. **What levels are tested to verify the AMR?**
    **Answer:** Low, mid and high across the range.

12. **What should be documented for a manual dilution?**
    **Answer:** Diluent, dilution factor, diluted result, final calculated result, tech, date/time.

## Case: a very high lipase

The analyser flags lipase as above range. Auto-dilution returns 3900 U/L with a flag saying the diluted result is still above range. The procedure says the maximum validated dilution is the analyser's auto-dilution. The ED physician calls asking for "the number".

**Questions**
1. Can you do a further manual dilution?
2. What do you report?
3. What do you tell the physician?
4. What do you document?

**Model answer**
1. Only if the lab has validated a further dilution for lipase. The procedure says no, so no.
2. "> [upper CRR limit] U/L" per the procedure.
3. The result exceeds the reportable range and is reported as greater than the upper limit. If a number is clinically essential, escalate to the supervisor or director per policy (e.g. send-out or a validated alternative).
4. The flags, auto-dilution result, reported value, physician call, escalation if any.
