## Learning objectives

1. Receive, store, label and load reagents correctly.
2. Track expiry and on-board / open-pack stability.
3. Explain why a new reagent lot must be verified before patient use, and why patient samples matter more than QC for this.
4. Perform and evaluate a lot-to-lot comparison against acceptance criteria.
5. Handle a new lot that fails verification.

---

## Lesson

### 1. Receiving and storage

On receipt, check: correct item, lot number, expiry, shipping temperature (indicators, cold packs, frozen vs refrigerated), damage. Record receipt date and lot. Store at the manufacturer's temperature. Rotate stock so the oldest usable lot goes first. Quarantine anything that arrived out of temperature until the supervisor decides.

Label opened reagents per procedure (date opened, new expiry, initials) where the analyser doesn't track this for you.

### 2. Expiry and stability

Three different clocks:

- **Manufacturer's expiry** on the box (unopened, stored correctly)
- **Open or on-board stability**: how long after opening or loading the reagent stays good
- **Calibration stability**: how long a calibration remains valid for that pack or lot

A reagent can be within its box expiry and still be past on-board stability. Most analysers track on-board time. Know how to check it and what happens when it runs out. **Never use expired reagents for patient testing.**

### 3. Why verify a new lot

Manufacturing lots differ slightly. Usually the difference doesn't matter. Sometimes it does, and a shift at a medical decision point can change patient management. A lot-to-lot verification shows the new lot gives patient results comparable to the current lot **before** it's put into use.

### 4. Why patient samples, not just QC

Control material is processed (stabilised, lyophilised, spiked). It may react differently to a reagent change than real patient samples do. This is called **non-commutability**. It means:

- QC can shift with a new lot when patients haven't, leading you to wrongly reject a good lot or re-target QC unnecessarily.
- QC can look fine while patients have shifted, which is the dangerous one.

So compare **patient samples** on old and new lots. Use QC as well, but don't rely on it alone. **[CHECK: the pilot lab's procedure; many follow CLSI EP26 for lot-to-lot evaluation.]**

### 5. Doing the comparison

1. Before the old lot runs out, load the new lot alongside (or on a second channel/analyser as your system allows). Calibrate the new lot if required.
2. Select patient samples covering the reportable range, with emphasis near **medical decision points**. **[CHECK: number of samples in the pilot lab's procedure, often 5–20 depending on the risk and the test.]**
3. Run each sample on both lots, close together in time.
4. Calculate the difference for each sample (new − old) in units and in percent.
5. Compare against the lab's **acceptance criteria** (a set allowable difference, often defined at decision points).
6. Run QC on the new lot and establish whether QC targets need adjusting (a supervisor decision).
7. Document, have it reviewed and signed, then release the lot.

### 6. When a new lot fails

- Repeat with fresh aliquots to exclude handling error.
- Check calibration of the new lot, calibrator values for that lot and reagent condition.
- If the difference is confirmed: **don't put the lot into use**. Notify the supervisor, quarantine the lot, contact the manufacturer, and check whether other labs have reported the same.
- If the old lot is about to run out, the supervisor/director decides the plan (another lot, backup method, send-out).

### 7. Records

Receipt log, storage temperature log, lot-to-lot worksheet with raw data, differences, acceptance criteria, pass/fail, reviewer signature and date the lot was placed in use.

## Question bank

**Pass mark: 80%. [CHECK]**

1. **A shipment of refrigerated reagents arrives warm. What do you do?**
   **Answer:** Don't put it into use. Quarantine it, document, notify the supervisor; contact the supplier per procedure.

2. **Name the three different "clocks" that limit how long a reagent can be used.**
   **Answer:** Manufacturer's expiry, open/on-board stability, calibration stability.

3. **Why is patient-sample comparison preferred over QC alone when verifying a new reagent lot?**
   **Answer:** QC material may be non-commutable. It can shift when patients don't, or stay flat when patients shift.

4. **Where should lot-to-lot patient samples be concentrated?**
   **Answer:** Across the range, with emphasis near medical decision points.

5. **Old lot glucose 126 mg/dL, new lot 136 mg/dL. Calculate the difference in units and percent.**
   **Answer:** +10 mg/dL; +7.9% (10 ÷ 126).

6. **A new lot fails acceptance criteria. Name three actions.**
   **Answer (any three):** repeat to exclude handling error; check calibration and calibrator values; quarantine; notify supervisor; contact manufacturer; plan backup.

7. **True or false: if QC is in range on the new lot, the patient comparison can be skipped.**
   **Answer:** False.

8. **A reagent is within its box expiry but past on-board stability. Can you use it?**
   **Answer:** No.

9. **Who decides whether QC target ranges change for a new reagent lot?**
   **Answer:** The supervisor or designee, per procedure, based on data.

10. **Why run old and new lots close together in time?**
    **Answer:** So differences reflect the lot, not specimen deterioration or instrument drift.

11. **What should the lot-to-lot record contain?**
    **Answer:** Lots, dates, samples and raw results, differences, acceptance criteria, pass/fail, reviewer, in-use date.

12. **All QC levels shift about +8% on the new lot but patient samples agree within 2%. What's the likely explanation?**
    **Answer:** Non-commutability of QC material with the new lot. Patient results are comparable; QC targets may need re-establishing per procedure (supervisor decision).

## Case: new glucose lot, +4%

You compare a new glucose reagent lot using 10 patient samples. Differences (new − old) average +4%, all in the same direction. At 126 mg/dL the difference is +5 mg/dL. The lab's acceptance criterion is ±5% or ±4 mg/dL at or below 100 mg/dL. **[CHECK: replace with the pilot lab's real criteria.]** QC on the new lot is about +0.5 SD on both levels. The old lot runs out in 36 hours.

**Questions**
1. Does the new lot pass?
2. Is there anything that should still worry you?
3. What would you do if the average had been +7%?
4. What do you document?

**Model answer**
1. On the stated criteria, yes: +4% average and +5 mg/dL (≈ +4%) at 126 are within ±5%.
2. The bias is consistent and uses most of the allowable difference. Watch QC and patient trends after release, and note it for the next lot. A consistent positive bias near the diagnostic threshold for diabetes (126 mg/dL fasting) deserves the supervisor's attention.
3. Fail. Repeat to confirm, check calibration and calibrator values, quarantine the lot, notify the supervisor, contact the manufacturer, and agree a plan before the old lot runs out.
4. The full worksheet: lots, raw results, differences, criteria, pass/fail, QC on the new lot, reviewer and in-use date.
