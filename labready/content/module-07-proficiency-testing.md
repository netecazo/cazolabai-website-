## Learning objectives

1. Explain what proficiency testing (PT) is for and why it's handled exactly like patient testing.
2. State what is prohibited: inter-lab communication and referral of PT samples.
3. Read a PT evaluation and recognise acceptable, unacceptable and ungraded results.
4. Investigate an unacceptable PT result and document corrective action.
5. Explain the consequences of repeated PT failure.

---

## Lesson

### 1. What PT is

PT is external quality assessment. An approved provider sends samples with unknown values a few times a year. The lab tests them and reports results, which are graded against peer groups or target values. It's the one regular check of your accuracy by someone outside the lab.

### 2. Treat PT like patients

PT samples must be tested **the same way as patient samples**:

- by the personnel who routinely do the testing, rotated among them
- with the same methods, instruments and number of replicates as patients
- integrated into the normal workload, not run specially by the best tech on a quiet day

The laboratory director and the testing personnel sign an **attestation** that the samples were handled this way.

### 3. What's prohibited

- **No inter-laboratory communication** about PT results before the provider's reporting deadline.
- **No referral** of PT samples, or any part of them, to another lab for testing, even if you'd normally send that test out. Intentional referral can lead to revocation of the lab's CLIA certificate.
- Don't run PT samples more times than you'd run a patient to "get it right". **[CHECK: confirm current wording against 42 CFR 493.801 and the pilot lab's accreditor.]**

If a PT sample is for a test you normally refer out, follow the provider's and your accreditor's instructions; usually you don't test it.

### 4. Reading the evaluation

- **Acceptable:** within the provider's limits.
- **Unacceptable:** outside the limits. Must be investigated and documented, even if the overall event score passes.
- **Ungraded / not evaluated:** e.g. no peer consensus, or result not received. The lab must still evaluate its own performance, for instance by comparing with peer data, and document it.

For most regulated analytes, an event score of at least 80% is satisfactory. Failing an event is **unsatisfactory**; failing the same analyte in two consecutive events, or two out of three, is **unsuccessful** performance and can lead to sanctions, including having to stop testing that analyte. **[CHECK: scoring thresholds and definitions against current CLIA and the pilot lab's provider.]**

### 5. Investigating an unacceptable result

Work through the categories and document each:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Clerical** | Transcription error, wrong units, wrong method code, results entered in the wrong row, missed deadline |
| **Methodological** | Method bias against peers, wrong peer group, known limitation of the method |
| **Technical / equipment** | QC or calibration problem at the time, reagent issue, instrument malfunction, pipetting error |
| **PT material** | Sample mishandled, reconstitution error, matrix effect with your method |
| **Unexplained** | No cause found after full investigation |

Steps:
1. Check the clerical side first. It's the most common cause.
2. Review QC, calibration, maintenance and reagent records for the day the PT was run.
3. If samples remain and stability allows, retest (for investigation only, not resubmission).
4. Look at patient results: if a technical cause is found, could patients have been affected? Look back as in Module 2.
5. Take corrective action and document it. The director or designee reviews and signs.

### 6. Records

Keep PT records (worksheets, instrument printouts, attestations, evaluations and corrective actions) per regulation and your accreditor, typically at least two years. **[CHECK: retention period in the pilot lab's policy.]**

## Question bank

**Pass mark: 80%. [CHECK]**

1. **Who should run PT samples?**
   **Answer:** The personnel who routinely perform the testing, rotated among them, as part of normal workflow.

2. **A colleague at another hospital calls to compare PT potassium results before the deadline. What do you do?**
   **Answer:** Decline. Inter-laboratory communication about PT before the deadline is prohibited.

3. **Your analyser is down. Can you send the PT samples to your sister lab to test?**
   **Answer:** No. Referral of PT samples is prohibited and can lead to certificate revocation. Contact the provider and your supervisor.

4. **Can you run a PT sample in triplicate and report the average if you run patients once?**
   **Answer:** No. PT is tested the same way as patients.

5. **Your event score for calcium was 80% but one sample was unacceptable. Do you need to investigate?**
   **Answer:** Yes. Every unacceptable result is investigated and documented.

6. **What's the difference between unsatisfactory and unsuccessful performance?**
   **Answer:** Unsatisfactory = failing one event. Unsuccessful = failing the same analyte in two consecutive events or two of three.

7. **What should be checked first when a PT result is unacceptable?**
   **Answer:** Clerical errors: transcription, units, method code, data entry.

8. **A result was "not graded" because there was no peer consensus. Is any action required?**
   **Answer:** Yes. The lab evaluates its own performance (e.g. against peer data) and documents it.

9. **Why does a PT failure potentially affect patients?**
   **Answer:** If the cause was technical, patient samples run at the same time were likely affected too; a look-back may be required.

10. **What does the attestation statement confirm?**
    **Answer:** That PT samples were tested in the same manner as patient samples, signed by the director and testing personnel.

11. **Name the five categories for classifying a PT failure.**
    **Answer:** Clerical, methodological, technical/equipment, PT material, unexplained.

12. **Can you correct a PT result and resubmit it after finding a clerical error post-deadline?**
    **Answer:** No. Document the error and corrective action; the graded result stands.

## Case: unacceptable PT magnesium

The PT evaluation arrives. Four of five magnesium samples are acceptable. Sample MG-03 is unacceptable: you reported 1.2, the target is 2.9 mg/dL. Your other results sit close to the peer mean. On the worksheet, MG-03 on the instrument printout reads 2.8 mg/dL.

**Questions**
1. What's the most likely category?
2. What else do you check?
3. Were patients at risk?
4. What corrective action would you document?

**Model answer**
1. Clerical. The printout (2.8) matches the target; the reported value (1.2) doesn't. Likely a transcription error or a value keyed from the wrong row.
2. Confirm which sample 1.2 belongs to (another analyte? another sample?), check the QC and calibration records for that day to rule out a technical cause, and check the other analytes on the same form.
3. Unlikely: the instrument result was correct, so patient testing wasn't affected.
4. Cause (transcription error), evidence (printout vs submitted), second-person verification of PT results before submission, staff reminder, director review and signature.
