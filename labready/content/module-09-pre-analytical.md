## Learning objectives

1. Verify specimen identification and apply acceptance and rejection criteria.
2. Explain how haemolysis, icterus and lipaemia (HIL) affect chemistry results and how to use serum indices.
3. Recognise the effects of wrong tube, order of draw, IV contamination and delayed processing.
4. Handle and document a rejected or compromised specimen.

## Lesson

### 1. Most errors happen before the analyser

Most laboratory errors happen in the pre-analytical phase: identification, collection, transport and processing. QC can't see any of them. Your checks at the bench are the last line of defence.

### 2. Identification

- Every specimen needs at least **two patient identifiers** that match the requisition or order.
- Unlabelled, mislabelled or mismatched specimens are handled per your procedure, usually rejected and recollected. Irreplaceable specimens (e.g. CSF) may follow a special process with documented accountability. **[CHECK: the pilot lab's mislabel and irreplaceable specimen policy.]**
- Never relabel a specimen yourself unless your procedure explicitly allows it.

### 3. Serum indices (HIL)

Most modern analysers measure haemolysis (H), icterus (I) and lipaemia (L) indices. Each assay has manufacturer-defined limits above which results are affected.

| Interferent | Common effects |
|---|---|
| **Haemolysis** | Falsely increases K, LD, AST, phosphate, Mg, iron; can affect some methods spectrally (e.g. bilirubin) |
| **Icterus** | Spectral and chemical interference, e.g. can falsely lower some creatinine and cholesterol methods |
| **Lipaemia** | Light scatter affects photometric assays; volume displacement causes **pseudohyponatraemia** with indirect ISE |

Follow your procedure: suppress the affected result, report with a comment, or treat the specimen (e.g. ultracentrifugation for lipaemia, if validated). **[CHECK: the pilot lab's HIL limits and actions per assay, from the manufacturer's inserts.]**

In-vivo haemolysis (true patient haemolysis) is clinically important. Repeated haemolysed samples from the same patient despite good collection technique should be raised with the clinical team.

### 4. Tubes and order of draw

- Wrong tube for the test: e.g. K from a potassium EDTA tube, glucose from a tube without a glycolysis inhibitor left unspun.
- **Order of draw** prevents additive carryover. A common sequence: blood cultures → citrate → serum (with or without clot activator/gel) → heparin → EDTA → fluoride/oxalate. **[CHECK: match to the pilot lab's collection manual.]**
- EDTA carryover pattern: high K, low Ca, low ALP (Module 8).

### 5. IV contamination

Specimens drawn above an infusion site are diluted with IV fluid:

- **Dextrose:** very high glucose, other analytes low.
- **Saline:** high Na and Cl, other analytes low.
- **Potassium-containing fluids:** high K.

Recollect from the opposite arm or below the site, per policy.

### 6. Processing and stability

- **Unspun samples:** glucose falls (glycolysis) at room temperature; potassium can rise over time, and cold storage of unspun blood also raises K by inhibiting the cell membrane pump.
- Separate serum/plasma within the time your procedure sets. **[CHECK]**
- Check clotting in plasma tubes and fibrin in serum. Clots and fibrin cause short sampling (falsely low results) and probe alarms.
- Respect analyte stability for add-on requests: some analytes aren't stable long enough to add on.

### 7. Rejection and documentation

When you reject or qualify a specimen, document: reason, who you notified, time, whether recollection was requested, and any comment added to the report.

## Question bank

**Pass mark: 80%. [CHECK]**

1. **How many patient identifiers must a specimen have?**
   **Answer:** At least two, matching the order.

2. **Name four analytes falsely increased by haemolysis.**
   **Answer (any four):** K, LD, AST, phosphate, Mg, iron.

3. **Sodium is 126 mmol/L on a grossly lipaemic sample measured by indirect ISE. What might be going on?**
   **Answer:** Pseudohyponatraemia from volume displacement. Confirm with direct ISE (e.g. blood gas analyser) or follow procedure.

4. **Why is order of draw important?**
   **Answer:** It prevents additive carryover between tubes (e.g. EDTA into a serum tube).

5. **A specimen drawn above a normal saline drip. What pattern?**
   **Answer:** High Na and Cl; other analytes diluted.

6. **A serum sample sat unspun at room temperature for five hours. What happens to glucose and potassium?**
   **Answer:** Glucose falls; potassium tends to rise.

7. **A specimen has only one identifier. What do you do?**
   **Answer:** Follow the mislabel procedure: usually reject and request recollection; document.

8. **What problems can fibrin in a serum sample cause?**
   **Answer:** Short sampling (falsely low results), probe clot alarms.

9. **What's the purpose of the HIL indices?**
   **Answer:** To objectively detect haemolysis, icterus and lipaemia and apply assay-specific limits.

10. **The same patient's samples are repeatedly haemolysed despite good collection. What should you consider?**
    **Answer:** In-vivo haemolysis. Raise it with the clinical team.

11. **What should be documented when a specimen is rejected?**
    **Answer:** Reason, who was notified, time, recollection requested, any report comment.

12. **Can you relabel a specimen the ward says is correct?**
    **Answer:** Only if your procedure explicitly allows it, and with the required documentation. Usually no.

## Case: glucose 900 from the ED

An ED sample: glucose 900 mg/dL, sodium 131, chloride 92, calcium 7.1 mg/dL, total protein 4.8 g/dL, BUN 6. Two hours ago, a sample from the same patient showed glucose 110, sodium 140, calcium 9.4, total protein 7.2. The patient isn't known to be diabetic. The nurse mentions the patient has an IV running.

**Questions**
1. What's the most likely explanation?
2. What do you do?
3. What if the clinicians insist the result is real?

**Model answer**
1. Contamination with dextrose IV fluid: very high glucose with everything else diluted, and a sharp delta from two hours ago.
2. Don't report the panel. Call the ED, explain the likely contamination, request a recollection from the opposite arm or with the infusion paused per policy. Document the call, the reason and the recollection.
3. Explain the evidence (the dilution pattern, the delta). A recollection settles it quickly. If there's disagreement, escalate to the supervisor or pathologist.
